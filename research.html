Research

How poor households in developing countries live with climate shocks — floods, heat, and salinity — and the frictions that constrain adaptation and labor mobility, studied with field experiments, administrative records, and long-run survey data from Bangladesh.

Job Market Paper

Who Bears the Loss from a Flood? Prices, Rationing, and Incidence in Rural BangladeshJob Market Paper

Floods are presumed to hit the poor through the wage. Following 5,473 rural Bangladeshi households matched to satellite flood maps, I show the loss falls instead on capital that the land-rich own: large landholders lose livestock and working capital, the labor market rations work rather than repricing it, and the land-poor adapt through self-employment. Who bears a flood follows who owns the capital it hits.

Draft (PDF) November 2026
Abstract

Floods destroy livestock, equipment and other productive assets across the developing world, and the usual presumption is that the poor bear the loss through a fall in the wage or labor rationing. This paper asks who bears it, through which village markets, and who adapts. I follow 5,473 rural households in Bangladesh's flood-prone delta from 2011 to 2019, matched to satellite flood maps. I compare households by the land they inherited, from none (the land-poor) to the most, within district and round. The presumption fails. The loss falls on capital, which the land-rich own. The largest inherited landholders lose livestock and working capital, and their working capital remains lower a window later. The loss is concentrated on cattle-holding farms: a flood raises the share that lose two or more head by 24 percentage points. Their farms shrink, and their wage and self-employment income falls by 7.6 percent of a year's income, most of it non-farm business income. The labor market rations work rather than repricing it. Per severe flood in the two years before an interview, farms hire a quarter of a year's harvest hours less. The land-poor lose nearly a quarter of their day labor, far more than any fall in the wage the data allow. Yet they adapt: they turn to self-employment. The same income measure falls by at most 4 percent of a year's income, their consumption by at most 0.05 percent, and their savings show no fall. The rent falls by a fifth, but no group rents in more land in total at the lower rent, and the distribution of operated land does not change. Labor can be redeployed; capital cannot. Who bears a flood follows who owns the capital it hits, not who is poor, so relief aimed at the poor would miss the farms that lose capital. In a model in which a farm without credit chooses when to replace lost animals, a grant to the farms that lose a herd lets them rebuild within a year and is worth about 2 percent of their consumption. A fairly priced index insurance that pays the full loss achieves the same rebuilding and about nine tenths of that welfare gain at no expected fiscal cost, since the farms pay its premium. The same budget spread over the land-poor moves no market.

Working Papers

The Role of Land Market in Achieving the Scale for Adaptation to Climate Change: Evidence from Bangladesh

Using census data on over one million farm households and a salinity discontinuity at the Bengal delta's hydrological boundary, salinity intrusion drives large reallocation from crops to aquaculture and a 52 percent rise in farm size, achieved through the rental market rather than land sales; religious diversity eases the transition and parcel fragmentation slows it.

PDF also available on SSRN
Abstract

This paper studies whether climate-induced shifts in land productivity can trigger structural adaptation in agriculture by overcoming long-standing frictions in land markets. In coastal Bangladesh, rising sea levels have pushed dry-season soil salinity beyond the threshold at which conventional crops survive. Using census data on over one million farm households and a fuzzy spatial regression discontinuity at the hydrological boundary between the tidally active and mature regions of the Bengal delta, I find that salinity above 12 dS/m moves 32 to 50 decimals of land per household from crops to aquaculture and raises average farm size by 52 percent. The transition runs through the rental market rather than land sales. Reallocation is faster in villages with greater religious diversity and slower where parcels are more fragmented.

Adapting to High Heat, Failing at the Extreme: Newborn Heat Mortality over Four Decades in Bangladesh

with Anoushka Chopra

Adaptation to heat is real but partial: across four decades of birth cohorts, neonatal mortality from 30–40°C days has fallen to zero, tied to rural electrification, while mortality from days above 40°C shows no decline.

PDF also available on SSRN
Abstract

Damage functions in climate economics typically assume that rising income and cooling technology steadily reduce the mortality cost of extreme heat. We test this with a birth-cohort decomposition for 276,031 Bangladeshi births from nine DHS rounds merged with ERA5 daily-maximum temperature. At 30–40°C, only the 1980 cohort shows a significant response; each later cohort's response is close to zero and significantly below it, and a heterogeneity-robust difference-in-differences estimator links the flattening to the staggered rollout of rural electrification, implying roughly 718,000 neonatal deaths averted. Above 40°C the effect is large for the 2010 cohort, about 43 additional deaths per 1,000 births per 10 additional days, and no test detects a decline. The tail effect implies 657 to 1,920 excess neonatal deaths per year by mid-century.

Heat and the Body: Acute Cardiometabolic Responses and the Measurement of Chronic Disease in Bangladesh

Household surveys diagnose chronic disease from a single measurement; matching 68,000 blood-pressure and glucose readings to the temperature at the hour they were taken shows they also measure the weather, and warmer fieldwork explains about a third of the recent rise in measured diabetes.

PDF
Abstract

Household surveys diagnose chronic disease from a single measurement of each respondent. Using 34,879 blood-pressure readings and 33,179 fasting glucose results from three Bangladesh DHS rounds, merged with ERA5 temperature at the date and hour of measurement, I test whether they also measure the weather. A one-degree rise in the daily maximum lowers systolic blood pressure on the day it is read by 0.56 mmHg and raises next-morning fasting glucose by 1.03 mg/dL; the response is to humid heat, and a fall in haemoglobin points to dilution rather than dehydration. Measured diabetes rises 0.63 and measured hypertension falls 0.84 percentage points per degree, while reported past diagnoses do not respond, and warmer 2022 fieldwork accounts for about a third of the 7.8-point rise in measured diabetes among adults aged 35 and over since 2017–18.

From Two Daughters to a Hurried Third: Son Preference, Birth Spacing, and Mortality

with Syed Manzoor Ahmed Hanifi

Son preference shortens birth intervals: mothers of two daughters conceive a third child sooner, raising the risk of neonatal death for that child by about 10 percentage points and implying roughly 35,000 excess neonatal deaths in Bangladesh over four decades.

PDF
Abstract

Son preference raises child mortality through sex selection, discriminatory investment and early weaning; we document a fourth channel, birth spacing. Bangladeshi mothers of two daughters conceive a third child sooner, compressing the interval below the WHO-recommended 24 months. Instrumenting a sub-24-month third conception with the sex composition of the first two births, we estimate a 10.2 percentage point higher risk of third-child neonatal death in Matlab and Chakaria surveillance data and 11.4 points in nine Bangladesh DHS rounds. The child harmed is the one the parents wanted, male with even probability. Nationally, this implies about 35,000 excess neonatal deaths over four decades.

Measuring Flood Damage to Pond Aquaculture: Satellite Evidence from Bangladesh

with Runa Laila

Matching a household panel to satellite-observed inundation, severe floods cut pond aquaculture yield by 48 percent and profit by about 18,500 taka, with no coping margin compensating; such floods cost the sector about 26 million dollars a year.

PDF
Abstract

We match three rounds of a Bangladeshi household panel to satellite-observed inundation to measure flood damage to pond aquaculture. Any inundation lowers the probability of operating a pond by 2.9 percentage points, a 16 percent relative decline. Severe floods, which inundate more than half the surrounding area, reduce yield by 48 percent, revenue by 47 percent and profit by about 18,500 taka. No coping margin compensates, and household income falls by more than the pond loss alone. Such floods cost the sector about 26 million dollars a year.

Ongoing Field Experiments and Data Collection

  • Strategies for Scaling Up International Migration in Underutilized Routes within Asia
    with Austin Davis, Cynthia Kinnan, Daliah Al-Shakhshir, and Ahmed Mushfiq Mobarak
    Randomized controlled trial on the Philippines–Japan migration corridor with Onodera User Run, a Japanese intermediary whose employer-financed model makes language and vocational training free to the migrant. Three randomizations: admission to training over roughly eleven quarterly cohorts, supplemental personal-initiative training among those admitted, and recruitment intensity across 98 barangays to identify spillovers.
  • OUR Japan Cohort Study
    with Austin Davis, Cynthia Kinnan, and Ahmed Mushfiq Mobarak
    Five-year panel of 750 Filipino migrants already working in Japan, drawn from five consecutive deployment cohorts, tracking earnings, savings, remittances, language, family life and well-being, with modules carried from a UAE–India migrant study for corridor comparison. Led the survey design.
  • Adapting to Drinking Water Scarcity in Coastal Bangladesh through Community-Level Water Desalination Business
    with Nahian Bin Khaled, Ahmed Mushfiq Mobarak, and Jared Stolove
    Pilot cluster-randomized trial of community-scale reverse osmosis plants under a social-enterprise model: 15 of 50 water-scarce communities receive a plant, about 100 households are surveyed per community, and the per-liter price each household pays is randomized across four levels by public lottery. Baseline October–November 2026.
  • Coastal Bangladesh Longitudinal Survey
    with Ahmed Mushfiq Mobarak, Dev Patel, and Nahian Bin Khaled · project lead
    Household and farm panel of roughly 4,500 households in 225 villages across Khulna, Bagerhat and Satkhira, sampled along the salinity gradient with soil and water salinity measured at village and plot level. First wave September–October 2026; annual follow-ups from 2027; linked to MIT JO-CREWSnet.
