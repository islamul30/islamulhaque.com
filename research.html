Research

I study how poor households live with climate change in the places where it has already arrived: who bears the cost of a flood, a heat wave or saltwater intrusion, how these shocks propagate through markets, what determines who can adapt, and whether labor mobility can serve both as adaptation and as a way out of poverty. Alongside household panels, birth histories, agricultural census and satellite data, I run field experiments and primary surveys in Bangladesh as a co-principal investigator.

Who Bears the Loss from a Flood? Propagation and Incidence Through Village MarketsJob Market Paper

Floods destroy productive assets, but who finally bears the loss depends on how village markets respond. I follow 5,473 households in Bangladesh, matched to satellite flood maps, and find that the largest landholders lose livestock and working capital, and their farms stay smaller for six years after the flood. The land-poor lose nearly a quarter of their wage labor days but make up most of it through self-employment. Labor can be redeployed quickly, while capital takes years to rebuild when credit is scarce.

Draft (PDF) November 2026
Abstract

Floods destroy livestock, equipment and other productive assets across the developing world, but the initial point of damage and the ultimate incidence can differ depending on how markets and households respond. This paper asks who bears the loss, through which village markets, and who adapts. I use a three-round panel of 5,473 households in Bangladesh from 2011 to 2019, matched to satellite flood maps, and compare neighboring Unions of the same district that floods cover unequally. The largest inherited landholders lose livestock and working capital, and their farms shrink. Their working capital and farm size both remain lower six years after the flood. The land-poor lose nearly a quarter of their wage labor days but turn to self-employment, so their combined income falls by at most 4 percent and their consumption by at most 0.05 percent. Labor can be redeployed quickly, while capital is slow to rebuild when credit is constrained. In a model, a grant to the farms that lose a herd lets them rebuild within two years instead of six; fairly priced index insurance does nearly as well.

Working Papers

The Role of Land Market in Achieving the Scale for Adaptation to Climate Change: Evidence from Bangladesh

Rising salinity in coastal Bangladesh pushes farmers from rice into aquaculture, which needs larger farms. Using census data on more than one million farm households and a spatial discontinuity at the delta's hydrological boundary, I find that salinity above 12 dS/m raises average farm size by 52 percent. The consolidation runs through the rental market, not land sales. It is faster in villages with greater religious diversity and slower where parcels are fragmented.

PDF also available on SSRN
Abstract

This paper studies whether climate-induced shifts in land productivity can trigger structural adaptation in agriculture by overcoming long-standing frictions in land markets. In coastal Bangladesh, rising sea levels have pushed dry-season soil salinity beyond the threshold at which conventional crops survive. Using census data on over one million farm households and a fuzzy spatial regression discontinuity at the hydrological boundary between the tidally active and mature regions of the Bengal delta, I find that salinity above 12 dS/m moves, on average, 32 decimals of land per farm from crops to aquaculture and raises average farm size by 52 percent. The transition runs through the rental market rather than land sales. Reallocation is faster in villages with greater religious diversity and slower where parcels are more fragmented.

Adapting to High Heat, Failing at the Extreme: Newborn Heat Mortality over Four Decades in Bangladesh

with Anoushka Chopra

Climate damage functions assume that rising income steadily lowers the mortality cost of heat. Across four decades of Bangladeshi births, the newborn mortality response to 30–40°C days falls to near zero after the 1980 cohort, a decline we link to rural electrification. Above 40°C we find no decline at all.

PDF also available on SSRN
Abstract

Damage functions in climate economics typically assume that rising income and cooling technology steadily reduce the mortality cost of extreme heat. We test this with a birth-cohort decomposition for 276,031 Bangladeshi births from nine DHS rounds merged with ERA5 daily-maximum temperature. At 30–40°C, only the 1980 cohort shows a significant response; each later cohort's response is close to zero and significantly below it. A heterogeneity-robust difference-in-differences estimator links the flattening to the staggered rollout of rural electrification, implying roughly 718,000 neonatal deaths averted. Above 40°C the effect is large for the 2010 cohort, about 43 additional deaths per 1,000 births per 10 additional days, and it shows no sign of flattening. The tail effect implies 657 to 1,920 excess neonatal deaths per year by mid-century.

Weather in the Data: Heat, Survey Biomarkers and the Measurement of Chronic Disease

Household surveys diagnose hypertension and diabetes from a single reading, taken on whatever day the field team arrives. Matching about 68,000 blood pressure and glucose readings in Bangladesh to the temperature when they were taken, I find that the readings also measure the weather. Warmer fieldwork explains about a third of the recent rise in measured diabetes, and because surveys record when each reading was taken, the error can be corrected in existing data.

PDF
Abstract

Household surveys diagnose chronic disease from a single biomarker reading per respondent, taken on whatever day the field team arrives. Using three rounds of the Bangladesh DHS merged with temperature at the date and hour of measurement, I find that a one-degree rise in the daily maximum lowers systolic blood pressure by 0.56 mmHg and raises fasting glucose by 1.03 mg/dL. Measured diabetes rises by 0.63 and measured hypertension falls by 0.84 percentage points per degree, while reported past diagnoses do not respond. The error is not classical: warmer fieldwork accounts for about a third of the 7.8-point rise in measured diabetes among adults aged 35 and over between 2017–18 and 2022.

From Two Daughters to a Hurried Third: Son Preference, Birth Spacing, and Mortality

with Syed Manzoor Ahmed Hanifi

We document a fourth way son preference raises child mortality: birth spacing. Mothers of two daughters conceive a third child sooner, which raises that child's risk of neonatal death by 10 to 11 percentage points. Nationally, this implies about 35,000 excess neonatal deaths in Bangladesh over four decades.

PDF
Abstract

Son preference raises child mortality through sex selection, discriminatory investment and early weaning; we document a fourth channel, birth spacing. Bangladeshi mothers of two daughters conceive a third child sooner, compressing the interval below the WHO-recommended 24 months. Instrumenting a sub-24-month third conception with the sex composition of the first two births, we estimate a 10.2 percentage point higher risk of third-child neonatal death in Matlab and Chakaria surveillance data and 11.4 points in nine Bangladesh DHS rounds. The child harmed is the one the parents hoped would be a son, and it is as likely to be a girl as a boy. Nationally, this implies about 35,000 excess neonatal deaths over four decades.

Measuring Flood Damage to Pond Aquaculture: Satellite Evidence from Bangladesh

with Runa Laila

Pond aquaculture feeds and employs millions, but open ponds are exposed to floods. Matching a household panel to satellite flood maps, we find that severe floods cut pond yield by 48 percent and profit by about 18,500 taka, and no coping margin makes up the loss. Severe floods cost the sector about 26 million dollars a year.

PDF
Abstract

We match three rounds of a Bangladeshi household panel to satellite-observed inundation to measure flood damage to pond aquaculture. Any inundation lowers the probability of operating a pond by 2.9 percentage points, a 16 percent relative decline. Severe floods, which inundate more than half the surrounding area, reduce yield by 48 percent, revenue by 47 percent and profit by about 18,500 taka. No coping margin compensates, and household income falls by more than the pond loss alone. Such floods cost the pond aquaculture sector, on average, 26 million dollars a year.

Ongoing Field Experiments and Data Collection

  • Strategies for Scaling Up International Migration in Underutilized Routes within Asia
    with Austin Davis, Cynthia Kinnan, Daliah Al-Shakhshir, and Ahmed Mushfiq Mobarak
    Randomized controlled trial on the Philippines–Japan migration corridor with Onodera User Run, a Japanese intermediary whose employer-financed model makes language and vocational training free to the migrant. Two randomizations: admission to training over roughly eleven monthly cohorts, and supplemental personal-initiative training among those admitted.
  • Adapting to Drinking Water Scarcity in Coastal Bangladesh through Community-Level Water Desalination Business
    with Nahian Bin Khaled, Jared Stolove, and Ahmed Mushfiq Mobarak
    Pilot cluster-randomized trial evaluating whether a community-based desalination business, run as a social enterprise, expands access to safe drinking water: plants randomized to 15 of 50 water-scarce communities, about 100 households surveyed per community.
  • Coastal Bangladesh Longitudinal Survey
    with Dev Patel, Nahian Bin Khaled, and Ahmed Mushfiq Mobarak
    Household and farm panel of roughly 4,500 households in 225 villages across Khulna, Bagerhat and Satkhira, sampled along the salinity gradient with soil and water salinity measured at village level. First wave, September–November 2026.
  • OUR Japan Cohort Study
    with Austin Davis, Cynthia Kinnan, Daliah Al-Shakhshir, and Ahmed Mushfiq Mobarak
    Five-year panel of 750 Filipino migrants already working in Japan, drawn from five consecutive deployment cohorts, tracking earnings, savings, remittances, language, family life, time use, social life and mental well-being.
